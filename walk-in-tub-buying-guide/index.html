1. Is a walk-in tub the right choice for you?

Start here, because the best walk-in tub in the world is the wrong purchase if a different setup would serve you better. A walk-in tub makes sense when someone still wants to bathe, not just shower, but climbing over a tub wall or getting up from the floor of a tub has become difficult or unsafe.

The bathroom deserves the attention. According to the CDC, more than 14 million adults 65 and older, about 1 in 4, report falling each year, and falls are the leading cause of injury in that age group. A CDC study of emergency department data found an estimated 234,094 nonfatal bathroom injuries among people 15 and older in 2008. About 81% were caused by falls, 37.3% happened while bathing, showering or getting out of the tub or shower, and injury rates were highest among people 85 and older.

A walk-in tub is usually a good fit when

  • Stepping over a standard tub wall feels unsteady, or someone has already slipped or nearly fallen getting in or out.
  • Sitting down on the floor of a tub and standing back up is difficult or impossible.
  • Warm soaking helps with sore muscles or stiff joints and the bather doesn't want to give it up.
  • The bather can walk short distances and step over a low threshold, with or without a grab bar.
  • You want a safer bathroom that still feels like a bathroom, not a clinical space.

A roll-in or walk-in shower may be better when

  • The bather uses a wheelchair full time or can't step over even a low threshold.
  • A caregiver needs room to stand beside the bather and help.
  • The bather prefers showering and rarely takes baths.
  • Sitting in the tub while it fills and drains would be uncomfortable or impractical.

Roll-in showers have a very low, barrier-free threshold that a wheelchair can roll across, and they pair well with a padded fold-up seat, grab bars and a handheld shower. We install those too, so we don't have a reason to push you toward one or the other. We just want you to get the setup that fits.

What about grab bars alone, or a tub cut-out?

For some people, a few well-placed grab bars, a non-slip mat and a sturdy bath bench solve the problem for now, and it's worth being honest about that. Converting a tub by cutting a low entry into the wall can also lower the step, but you give up soaking depth, because the tub can no longer fill above the cut. A walk-in tub is the choice when you want both a low, safe entry and a full, deep bath.

Good idea before you decide: ask your doctor or physical therapist for a referral to an occupational therapist for a home safety evaluation. They can assess how the bather moves and recommend the right bathing setup. We're glad to build around their recommendations.

2. Start with who will bathe, not with the tub

The single biggest mistake we see is choosing a tub from photos before thinking about the person who will use it every day. Before you look at models, write down a few honest answers.

  • Height and build. Taller bathers need enough interior length and depth to soak comfortably. Larger bathers need enough seat width and door opening width to sit and move without squeezing.
  • Mobility today. Can the bather step over a low threshold? Do they use a cane, walker or wheelchair? Can they turn and sit without help?
  • Mobility in 5 to 10 years. Many of our Houston customers are planning ahead. A progressive condition, an upcoming joint replacement or a spouse's changing needs can all change the right answer.
  • Strength and reach. Can the bather comfortably reach and operate a faucet, drain lever and door latch while seated? Arthritis in the hands makes lever handles and simple controls important.
  • Heat tolerance. Some people get lightheaded in a hot bath, and some medications affect blood pressure. That affects how long a soak should be and how important temperature control is.
  • Caregiver involvement. If someone helps with bathing, they need space to assist and a door style that makes helping easier.
  • Who else uses the bathroom. If this is the only tub in the home, think about how grandkids, guests or a future buyer will use it too.

Once you know those answers, the rest of the decisions get much simpler. Most of the sections below come back to them.

Talking with a parent about a walk-in tub

Many of the calls we get come from adult children worried about a mom or dad who lives alone. That conversation can be delicate. For a lot of older adults, a walk-in tub can feel like an admission of getting older, and nobody likes being told what they can no longer do.

It usually goes better when the conversation is about independence rather than limitation. A walk-in tub is what lets someone keep bathing on their own, in their own home, on their own schedule, without needing help or giving up a bath they enjoy. Ask what they find hard right now instead of listing what worries you. Involve them in choosing the door style, the seat and the comfort features so it's their bathroom, not a decision made for them.

It can also help to hear it from a neutral voice. A doctor, physical therapist or occupational therapist can explain the fall risk in plain terms, and we're happy to talk directly with the person who'll be bathing so they can ask their own questions without feeling rushed.

3. Measuring your bathroom the right way

A walk-in tub has to do three things physically: fit through your home on the way in, fit the space where it's going, and connect to the plumbing and electrical that are already there or can reasonably be added. Measuring for all three before you choose a model prevents the most expensive surprises.

Measure the space where the tub will go

  • Length: measure the alcove wall to wall at the floor, in the middle and near the top of the old tub. Walls are rarely perfectly straight, so use the smallest number.
  • Width: measure from the back wall to the front edge of the existing tub.
  • Height: note any windows, soap niches or shelves that might interfere with a taller walk-in tub.
  • Drain side: stand facing the tub and note whether the drain is on the left or right. Walk-in tubs are usually made in left-drain and right-drain versions, and matching your existing drain keeps installation simpler.

Measure the path into the bathroom

Measure the width of every door and hallway between the front door and the bathroom, including the bathroom door itself, and note tight turns. A walk-in tub is taller than a standard tub, so a narrow doorway or a tight corner can matter. This is one of the first things our team checks, because a tub that fits the alcove but can't get through the house doesn't help anyone.

Check the clearance in front of the tub

If you're considering an outward swing door, measure the open floor space beside and in front of the tub. The door needs room to swing open fully, and the bather needs room to stand, turn and sit. A toilet, vanity or bathroom door that sits close to the tub can rule out an outward swing door or make one the better choice for access.

Look at the utilities

  • Where is the water heater, how old is it and what size is it? More on why this matters in section 7.
  • Is there a nearby electrical circuit, and is there room in the electrical panel for a new dedicated circuit if you want jets or a heater?
  • Is the bathroom on a concrete slab or over a crawl space, and is it on the ground floor or upstairs?

Don't worry if you can't answer all of this yourself. When you choose full installation, we measure and check all of it for you. Having rough numbers ready just makes the first phone call faster.

4. Sizes, seat height and water depth

Walk-in tubs come in a range of footprints. The right size balances three things: what fits your space, what fits the bather's body and how much water you're willing to heat and wait for.

Compact and alcove-sized tubs

Compact footprints such as 30 x 52 inches and 28 x 51 inches are made to drop into many existing tub alcoves with little or no framing change, which is why they're popular for straight tub-to-walk-in-tub replacements. A smaller tub also holds less water, so it can fill and drain faster and asks less of your water heater.

Full-length tubs

Longer tubs, including models around 60 inches long, can fill the full width of a standard alcove and give more leg room. Some are designed for more reclined bathing. They typically hold more water, so check hot water capacity and drain speed carefully.

Wide and bariatric tubs

Wider tubs offer more seat width and a wider door opening for larger bathers or anyone who wants more room to move. They need more floor space and more water, and they can be harder to bring through narrow hallways, so measure the path carefully.

Seat height and water depth

The seat is where walk-in tubs earn their value. A seat close to standard chair height makes sitting and standing much easier than lowering yourself to a tub floor. At the same time, the water needs to come up high enough on the seated bather to feel like a real soak, usually up around the chest or shoulders. When you compare models, look at the manufacturer's seat height and water depth together, and think about the bather's height. A taller bather on a high seat may want deeper water.

Not sure which size fits? Send us your measurements and who will be bathing, and we'll recommend sizes that make sense for your space.

5. Door styles: inward, outward and transfer

The door is the defining feature of a walk-in tub, and the style you choose changes how easy the tub is to use and how much floor space it needs.

Inward swing doors

An inward swing door opens into the tub. Because it doesn't swing into the room, it works well in smaller bathrooms where a toilet or vanity sits close to the tub. When the tub is full, water pressure pushes the door against its seal. The trade-off is that the open door takes up some of the space inside the tub, so the bather steps in around it, and the door opening can feel narrower.

Outward swing doors

An outward swing door opens into the bathroom, which usually makes for a wider, less obstructed entry. It's often easier for someone using a walker to approach, and easier for a caregiver to stand close and help. It needs clear floor space beside the tub for the door to swing fully open, so it isn't the right fit for every room.

Wheelchair transfer doors

Transfer tubs use a wide outward door and a seat positioned near the opening so a bather can slide over from a wheelchair or transfer bench and then swing their legs inside. You sit down to enter instead of stepping up and over. For bathers who can transfer but can't step, this is often the most practical walk-in tub design.

What to look for in any door

  • A latch you can work while seated, with a large lever or handle that doesn't require a tight grip.
  • A replaceable seal, so a worn gasket can be serviced instead of replacing the door.
  • A clear opening width that suits the bather's body and any mobility aid.
  • Sturdy hinges and frame that feel solid when the door opens and closes.

Remember the basic rule: a walk-in tub door can't be opened while there's water in the tub. You close the door before filling and wait for the tub to drain before opening it. That's normal for every walk-in tub and is why drain speed matters.

6. Threshold height, seat, grab bars and controls

Step-in threshold

The threshold is the low lip you step over at the door. It replaces the tall wall of a standard tub, and lower is generally easier. Compare the threshold height on the models you're considering and picture the bather stepping over it with a cane or walker. If even a low step is a challenge, look at a transfer tub or a roll-in shower instead.

Seat design

Look for a molded seat with a textured or contoured surface so you don't slide. The seat should be deep enough to sit back comfortably and wide enough for the bather. Some tubs offer a contoured seat or a slightly reclined back for comfort during a longer soak.

Grab bars

Built-in grab bars give the bather something solid to hold while sitting, standing and stepping out. Check that they're positioned where the bather's hands naturally go when rising from the seat and when stepping over the threshold. With full installation, we can also add wall-mounted grab bars outside the tub where the bather needs them.

Controls within reach

Faucet handles, drain controls and jet buttons should be reachable from the seat without leaning or twisting. Large lever handles and simple, clearly marked buttons are easier for hands with arthritis or reduced grip strength.

Handheld shower

A handheld shower wand lets the bather rinse hair and body from the seat, rinse the tub after bathing and helps a caregiver when assistance is needed. Check the hose length and where the holder mounts.

7. Filling, draining and hot water

This is the part of walk-in tub ownership that surprises people most, so it's worth understanding before you buy. Because the door must be closed before the tub fills and can't open until it drains, the bather sits inside through the whole cycle.

Fill time

How fast a tub fills depends on the size of the tub, the faucet design and your home's water pressure. A fast-fill faucet can shorten the wait noticeably. Many people fill the tub a little at a time while seated, adjusting the temperature as they go, so they stay comfortable.

Drain time

Drain speed is one of the most important comfort features on a walk-in tub, because the bather is sitting in a cooling tub while it empties. Drain systems vary between models, with some designs using larger drains or powered drain assistance to speed things up. Ask how long a full tub takes to drain and what drives that number. We'll help you compare.

Staying warm while draining

Some tubs offer heated seats or heated backrests that help the bather stay warm while the tub fills and drains. A warm bathroom, a towel draped over the shoulders and rinsing with the handheld shower while the water goes down all help too.

Water heater capacity

Walk-in tubs are deep, and a deep tub needs a lot of hot water. A tank water heater doesn't deliver its entire rated capacity as hot water, because cold water flows in as hot water flows out and the mix cools. If the tub holds more hot water than your heater can supply at once, the bath runs lukewarm. Before choosing a large tub, check:

  • The size and age of your water heater and whether anyone else uses hot water at bath time.
  • Whether a larger tank, a second heater, a tankless unit or a more compact tub would solve a shortfall.
  • For tankless water heaters, whether the unit's flow rate can keep up with a fast-fill faucet on a cool day.

When we quote a full installation, we look at your water heater as part of the job so you know ahead of time whether it's a fit.

8. Hydrotherapy and comfort options

Jets and comfort features can make bath time something to look forward to. They also add cost, components and electrical needs, so choose the ones you'll actually use.

Soaking tubs (no jets)

A soaking tub is the simplest design, with nothing to maintain beyond regular cleaning and no pumps that need a dedicated circuit. If the bather mainly wants a safe, warm bath, a soaking tub is a strong and often overlooked choice.

Air jets

Air jets push warm air through small openings, surrounding the bather with gentle bubbles. The sensation is soft and full-body. Air systems generally don't hold standing water in their lines the way some water jet systems can, and many use a purge cycle to blow the lines dry after the bath.

Water jets (whirlpool)

Water jets recirculate bath water through adjustable nozzles for a stronger, more targeted massage, often positioned for the back, hips, legs and feet. People who like a firm massage for sore muscles tend to prefer them. Because water passes through the plumbing, follow the manufacturer's cleaning instructions for the jet lines.

Dual systems

Dual systems combine air and water jets and let the bather choose. They offer the most variety and the most components, so they make sense if you'll really use both.

Other comfort features

  • Inline water heaters help keep bath water warm during a longer soak with jets running.
  • Heated seat and backrest options help the bather stay comfortable while filling and draining.
  • Colored lighting and other extras are available on some tubs. They're nice to have, not safety features.

About health claims: warm water and massage can feel soothing, but a walk-in tub isn't a medical treatment. If you have heart disease, blood pressure concerns, diabetes, circulation problems or you're pregnant, ask your doctor about safe water temperatures and soak times before using hot water or jets.

9. Materials and build quality

Most walk-in tubs are made from acrylic or from fiberglass with a gelcoat surface. Both can make a good tub when they're well built.

  • Acrylic is a nonporous material with color that runs through the sheet, and it's typically reinforced from behind. It resists staining, holds heat well and can often be buffed to remove minor surface scratches.
  • Gelcoat fiberglass uses a molded fiberglass shell with a smooth gelcoat finish on the surface. It's widely used and practical, and quality depends heavily on how thick and well applied the gelcoat and reinforcement are.

Beyond the shell, look at the frame or support system under the tub, how the door frame is built, whether the pumps and valves are easy to service, and whether an access panel lets a technician reach them later. A tub that can be serviced is a tub that lasts.

10. Safety features and code items to ask about

Scald protection

Older adults can have thinner skin and slower reaction times, which makes hot water burns a real risk. Ask for an anti-scald or thermostatic mixing valve that limits the maximum water temperature coming into the tub. Plumbing codes in many areas require temperature-limiting valves on bathtub fillers, and your installer should set the maximum temperature correctly during installation.

GFCI-protected electrical

Jetted tubs, inline heaters and heated seats need electrical power near water, so they require proper electrical service, typically a dedicated GFCI-protected circuit installed to code. Never plug a jetted tub into an extension cord or an existing bathroom outlet that wasn't designed for it.

Slip resistance

Check that the floor of the tub is textured and that the bathroom floor outside the tub is safe when wet. A good bath mat with a non-slip backing, or slip-resistant flooring, helps where the bather steps out.

Permits and inspections

Depending on your city and the scope of work, plumbing or electrical changes may require a permit. Ask your installer who is responsible for permits and inspections so there's no confusion. When we handle your installation, we'll tell you what your project needs.

11. Houston home factors that change the plan

Every region has its own building patterns, and a few come up often in the Houston area.

Slab foundations

Many Houston-area homes are built on concrete slabs. Over a slab, the drain line is set in concrete, so moving a drain means cutting and patching the slab. That's why matching the new tub's drain side and location to your existing drain is usually the most practical path, and why we check it before recommending a model.

Two-story homes and upstairs bathrooms

A filled walk-in tub with a person in it is heavy. For a second-floor bathroom, the floor framing needs to handle the tub's filled weight, so we look at the structure before recommending a model and let you know if reinforcement makes sense.

Humidity and ventilation

Houston's humid climate and a deep, warm bath add up to a lot of moisture in the bathroom. A properly sized exhaust fan that vents outside helps control moisture, protects walls and ceilings and helps prevent mold and mildew. If your bathroom doesn't have one, it's worth considering during the project.

Older plumbing

In older homes, the valves and supply lines behind the wall may be due for replacement. It's far easier and less costly to update them while the wall is open for the new tub than to reopen it later.

Let us check your home for you. Slab, second floor, water heater, electrical, plumbing: we look at all of it before you buy.

12. Installation: full service or DIY

A walk-in tub is only as good as its installation. The tub has to sit level so it drains completely, the door has to seal, the valve has to be set to a safe maximum temperature, and any electrical work has to be done correctly. That's why we recommend full installation for most customers.

How long it takes

A straightforward swap of a standard tub for a walk-in tub is often completed in one to two days once the tub is on site. Plumbing changes, a new electrical circuit, wall repairs or a new exhaust fan can add time. Your quote should include a realistic timeline.

Supply-only for experienced DIYers and contractors

If you're comfortable with plumbing, framing and finish work, or you have a contractor you trust, you can buy a supply-only walk-in tub. We include tech support on supply-only orders so you have someone to call. Keep in mind that any electrical work for jets or heaters should be done by a licensed electrician, and that your manufacturer's warranty may have installation requirements, so read them before you start.

Questions to ask any installer

  • Who exactly will be doing the installation, and are they trained on this type of tub?
  • Does the quote include demo and haul-away, plumbing, electrical, surround and cleanup?
  • Who handles permits and inspections if they're needed?
  • What happens if something unexpected turns up behind the wall?
  • What warranty covers the labor, and for how long?
  • Who do I call if I have a problem after installation?

A good installer answers all of these clearly and in writing. If you get vague answers or pressure to sign the same day, take your time.

13. What drives the cost, and ways to pay

We don't publish one-size-fits-all prices, because an accurate quote depends on your tub and your home. Understanding what affects the price helps you compare quotes fairly and decide where to spend.

What affects the price

  • Tub size and style. Compact soaking tubs, full-length tubs, wide models and transfer tubs are priced differently.
  • Hydrotherapy and comfort options. Air jets, water jets, dual systems, inline heaters and heated seats add cost.
  • Door and faucet features, such as outward swing doors or fast-fill faucets.
  • Removal of the old tub or shower and any repairs to walls or flooring underneath.
  • Plumbing changes, especially if a drain has to move on a slab foundation.
  • Electrical work for a dedicated circuit for jets or heaters.
  • Water heater upgrades if your current heater can't fill the tub.
  • Surround and finishing work, such as wall panels around the tub.

When comparing quotes, make sure they cover the same scope. A low number that leaves out removal, electrical or the surround can end up costing more. For a fast, accurate quote for your home, call 1-888-779-2284 (BATH).

Medicare

Original Medicare (Parts A and B) generally doesn't pay for walk-in tubs, because bathroom safety equipment isn't usually treated as covered durable medical equipment. Some Medicare Advantage plans offer supplemental benefits for bathroom safety items, but coverage varies by plan and area. Call your plan and ask exactly what's covered. You can review Medicare's durable medical equipment coverage.

VA HISA grant

The VA's Home Improvements and Structural Alterations (HISA) grant is a lifetime benefit for medically necessary home modifications. According to VA, it provides up to $6,800 when the project addresses a service-connected disability, or a non-service-connected disability when the veteran also has a service-connected disability rated at least 50%, and up to $2,000 for other eligible disabilities. Every HISA project must be medically justified, and the process runs through VA's Prosthetic and Sensory Aids Service, so start with your VA health care team.

Texas Amy Young Barrier Removal Program

The Texas Department of Housing and Community Affairs offers the Amy Young Barrier Removal Program, which provides one-time grants of up to $22,500 for persons with disabilities who need accessibility modifications or need hazardous conditions removed. Household income can't exceed 80% of the area median family income, both tenants and homeowners may qualify, and the program is run through local nonprofits and governments, so it isn't available everywhere.

Medical expense tax deduction

IRS Publication 502 explains that home improvements made primarily for medical care can be included as medical expenses, generally to the extent the cost exceeds any increase in the home's value, and it lists modifications such as grab bars and bathroom changes made for a person with a disability. Rules and income limits apply. Read IRS Publication 502 and talk with a tax professional.

Local help in the Houston region

The Harris County Area Agency on Aging, run through the Houston Health Department, offers benefits counseling and caregiver support for adults 60 and older. The Houston-Galveston Area Agency on Aging serves the surrounding counties, and 2-1-1 Texas can connect you with local assistance programs anywhere in the state.

15. Living with a walk-in tub

A safe bathing routine

This is a sequence worth practicing with the bather after installation:

  1. Set out a towel, robe and anything needed within reach before starting.
  2. Open the door, hold the grab bar, step in and sit down.
  3. Close and latch the door.
  4. Start filling, checking the temperature with your hand before sitting back.
  5. Bathe, using the handheld shower as needed.
  6. Open the drain and stay seated until the tub is empty.
  7. Unlatch the door, hold the grab bar and stand slowly. Pause a moment before stepping out, especially after a warm bath.

Cleaning and care

  • Rinse and wipe the tub after use to prevent soap buildup.
  • Use non-abrasive cleaners that are safe for your tub's surface.
  • Follow the manufacturer's instructions for cleaning water jet lines and running air purge cycles.
  • Check the door seal periodically for wear, and wipe it clean so it seals properly.
  • Keep the bathroom ventilated during and after baths to control moisture.

When to call for service

Call for help if you notice water on the floor near the door, the door becoming hard to latch, slow draining that doesn't improve after cleaning the drain, or jets or a heater that stop working. Small fixes like a worn seal are much easier when caught early.

17. Walk-in tub buying guide FAQ

How do I know what size walk-in tub I need?

Start with the space and the bather. Measure the alcove length and width, the path through your home and the drain side, then consider the bather's height, build and seat width needs. Compact footprints such as 30 x 52 inches and 28 x 51 inches fit many standard tub alcoves, while longer and wider models offer more room. We can recommend sizes once we have your measurements.

What's the difference between a walk-in tub and a regular bathtub?

A walk-in tub has a watertight door with a low step-in threshold instead of a tall side wall, a built-in seat at about chair height, a textured floor and grab bars. You sit to bathe instead of lowering yourself to the tub floor. The trade-off is that you sit inside while the tub fills and drains.

Can a walk-in tub door leak?

A properly installed walk-in tub with a good door seal shouldn't leak. Leaks usually come from a worn or dirty seal, a door that isn't fully latched or a tub that wasn't installed level. Keep the seal clean, check it periodically and call for service if you notice water near the door.

Are air jets or water jets better?

It depends on what feels good to the bather. Air jets create gentle bubbles across the body, and air systems generally don't hold standing water in their lines. Water jets give a stronger, targeted massage but use recirculated bath water, so their lines need regular cleaning. A soaking tub with no jets is also a great option if safety and simplicity matter most.

Do I need a bigger water heater for a walk-in tub?

Maybe. Walk-in tubs are deep, and a tank water heater doesn't deliver its full rated capacity as hot water. If your tub holds more hot water than your heater can supply, baths can run lukewarm. We check your water heater during the quote and explain your options if it may come up short.

Can I install a walk-in tub myself?

Experienced DIYers and contractors can install a supply-only walk-in tub, and we include tech support on supply-only orders. The tub must be level, the door must seal, the valve needs a safe maximum temperature and any electrical work for jets or heaters should be done by a licensed electrician. Most customers choose our full installation so everything is handled.

What is a wheelchair transfer tub?

A transfer tub has a wide outward swing door and a seat positioned near the opening so the bather can slide over from a wheelchair or transfer bench, then swing their legs inside. You sit down to enter instead of stepping over a threshold.

Is a walk-in tub safe for someone with heart or blood pressure problems?

Warm water and jets can feel soothing, but hot baths can affect blood pressure and make some people lightheaded. Anyone with heart disease, blood pressure concerns, diabetes, circulation problems or who is pregnant should ask their doctor about safe water temperatures and soak times before using a walk-in tub.

Should I choose a walk-in tub or a roll-in shower?

Choose a walk-in tub if the bather wants to soak and can step over a low threshold or transfer onto a seat. Choose a roll-in shower if the bather uses a wheelchair full time, needs a caregiver standing beside them or prefers showering. We install both and will help you choose based on how the bather moves today and in the future.

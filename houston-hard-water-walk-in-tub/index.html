Water and upkeep

Houston hard water and your walk-in tub

Most Houston tap water is hard. That will not ruin a walk-in tub, but it does change how you care for the door seal, the jets and the water heater that fills it.

Direct answer: Yes, Houston water is hard. The City of Houston's 2025 water quality report lists an average of 135.5 mg/L of hardness for its main system, in the U.S. Geological Survey's "hard" range. For a walk-in tub, that means wiping the door seal after baths, cleaning jets with a manufacturer-approved cleaner and flushing sediment from the water heater.

What the city's own numbers say

Every water utility has to publish a yearly water quality report. Houston Public Works runs six public water systems, and its 2025 report lists total hardness for each one. Hardness is the amount of dissolved calcium and magnesium, reported in milligrams per liter as calcium carbonate. To convert to grains per gallon, the unit many softener and appliance makers use, divide by about 17.1.

The main system's range is wide because Houston blends surface water from Lake Houston, Lake Conroe and Lake Livingston with groundwater from the Chicot and Evangeline aquifers. The hardness at your tap depends partly on which plants serve your part of town.

Outside Houston city limits

Katy, Cypress, Spring, Sugar Land, Pearland, The Woodlands and most suburbs get water from their own city utility or a municipal utility district (MUD), not from Houston. Each one publishes its own water quality report, usually on its website or printed with a summer bill. Look for the line "Total Hardness as CaCO3." If your utility does not list it, call and ask; it is a routine question.

Where hard water shows up on a walk-in tub

A walk-in tub has more moving and sealing parts than a standard bathtub, and that is where minerals cause trouble first.

  • The door gasket. White scale left on the seal can keep it from seating evenly. Wiping the gasket dry after each bath and cleaning it gently as the manual directs is the single most useful habit.
  • Faucet and diverter. Scale slows the fill rate over time. Because you sit in the tub while it fills, a slow faucet means a longer, cooler wait.
  • Quick-drain parts. Mineral buildup in the drain mechanism can slow draining. Ask how to reach and clean it before you buy.
  • Water jets. Whirlpool lines hold water between uses. Minerals and soap residue can collect there, so run the cleaning cycle the owner's manual describes. Air jets blow their lines dry, which helps.
  • Handheld shower. Spray nozzles clog with scale. Rubber nozzles that you can rub clean are easier than metal ones.
  • Surface film. Hard water and soap leave a dull film on acrylic and gelcoat. Use cleaners the manufacturer approves; abrasive powders scratch the finish and make the next film stick harder.

Check the warranty wording. Some manufacturer warranties exclude damage from mineral buildup or from using the wrong cleaners. Ask for the written warranty and the care instructions before you choose a model, and keep them with the house papers.

The water heater matters as much as the tub

A walk-in tub uses more hot water than a standard bath, so it leans hard on the water heater. In a tank heater, minerals settle to the bottom as sediment. The U.S. Department of Energy's Energy Saver guide suggests draining a quart of water from a storage tank every three months to clear sediment that slows heating and wastes energy. Follow your heater's own manual first, and if the heater is in a hot attic, as many Houston heaters are, have someone who can manage the ladder do it.

Tankless heaters collect scale too, and their manuals usually describe a descaling flush for hard-water areas. Either way, a heater that is already struggling will struggle more to fill a deep tub. Should you add a water softener?

A softener is not required for a walk-in tub, and it is a whole-house decision with its own upkeep: salt or potassium, regeneration water and a place to put the unit. It can make sense if you already see heavy scale on faucets, shower glass and the dishwasher. It may not be worth it for a home where a weekly wipe-down keeps things clean.

A few points to weigh:

  • Softened water adds sodium. People on a low-sodium diet often keep one unsoftened tap for drinking and cooking. Ask the person's doctor if that applies.
  • Softener installation that ties into the water supply is plumbing work. In Texas, plumbers are licensed by the Texas State Board of Plumbing Examiners, and a license can be checked on the board's website.

A simple upkeep routine for a Houston walk-in tub

  1. After each bath, let the tub drain fully, wipe the door gasket and leave the door slightly open so it dries.
  2. Once a week, clean the shell, faucet and handheld nozzles with a cleaner the manufacturer approves.
  3. Run the jet purge or cleaning cycle as often as the manual says.
  4. Every few months, look closely at the door seal for white crust, cracks or flattening, and check that the tub still drains at its normal speed.
  5. Every three months, drain sediment from a tank water heater, or schedule it with a plumber.

Questions we hear about this

Is Houston water hard?

Yes, in most of the city. The City of Houston's 2025 water quality report lists an average total hardness of 135.5 mg/L as calcium carbonate for its main system, which falls in the "hard" range on the U.S. Geological Survey scale. Readings ranged from 43.5 to 229 mg/L.

Does hard water damage a walk-in tub?

It does not usually harm the tub shell, but mineral scale can build up on the door seal, faucet, drain parts, jets and the water heater. Regular wiping, cleaning with products the manufacturer approves and occasional checks keep those parts working.

Do I need a water softener for a walk-in tub?

Not always. A softener is a whole-house choice with its own costs and upkeep. Many families simply clean the door gasket and jets on the schedule in the owner's manual. If you already see heavy white scale on faucets and glass, ask a licensed plumber about treatment options.

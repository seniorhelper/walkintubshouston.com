Planning an accessible bathroom for Houston floods, storms and humidity

Harvey, Imelda and Beryl taught Houston families hard lessons. A few choices made now can keep a safer bathroom usable when the weather turns.

Why weather belongs in a bathroom decision

Most bathroom guides are written for places where the biggest risk is a leaky faucet. In the Houston region, the list also includes street and river flooding, hurricane wind and rain, long power outages in summer heat, and humidity that never really leaves. None of this means you should skip a walk-in tub or roll-in shower. It means you should plan the details with these conditions in mind.

Flooding: know your history and your map

The Harris County Flood Control District's final report on Hurricane Harvey counted about 154,170 single-family homes flooded in the county in 2017, according to local coverage of that report, with only about a third carrying flood insurance. Many of those homes flooded for the first time. Since then, flood maps and local rules have changed.

  • Check the FEMA flood map for your address at the FEMA Map Service Center, and look at the Harris County Flood Control District's resources for your watershed.
  • Know the City of Houston rule for big projects. After Harvey, the city updated Chapter 19 of its code so that new construction and substantial improvements in the 500-year floodplain must be built two feet above that floodplain elevation. A tub swap is not usually a substantial improvement, but a larger remodel or addition might be. Ask the Houston Permitting Center if you are planning more than a bathroom refresh.
  • If the house flooded before, tell your installer. We look harder at the subfloor and wall cavity behind the tub, and we can talk about materials that tolerate water better, such as solid surround panels instead of paper-faced drywall.

Hurricanes and power outages

When Hurricane Beryl hit in July 2024, more than two million CenterPoint customers lost power at the peak, and many waited days in high heat. A bathroom that depends on electricity is worth thinking through.

What still works without power

  • The door, seat and grab bars. These are mechanical and work in the dark.
  • Gravity draining. Walk-in tubs drain by gravity. Some quick-drain systems use a pump; ask whether the model you choose drains, more slowly, without it.
  • Hot water from a gas heater, if the heater does not need electric ignition. Many newer gas heaters do, so check yours.

What stops

  • Air and water jets, heated seats and in-line heaters.
  • Electric water heaters and tankless units.
  • Exhaust fans and lights, which matters for a person who needs light to bathe safely.

Practical steps: keep a battery lantern or motion-sensor night light in the bathroom, know how to bathe safely with the jets off, and if the person bathing depends on warm water for comfort, include that in your family's storm plan. Ready.gov's hurricane checklist is a good starting point for the rest of the house.

Humidity, mold and ventilation

The EPA advises keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent, ideally between 30 and 50 percent, to limit mold growth. In a Houston bathroom that means more than opening a window.

  • Run a properly sized exhaust fan that vents outside, not into the attic, during and after every bath. A timer switch helps.
  • Seal the surround well. Walk-in tubs have more seams around the door and deck than a standard tub. Clean, continuous sealant lines keep water out of the wall.
  • Dry the door seal. After a bath, wiping the gasket and leaving the door ajar helps it last.
  • Pick jets that drain. Air systems blow out their lines; ask how a water-jet system purges standing water, since stale water in lines is what grows things.
  • Watch the floor. A shower with a poor slope leaves standing water, which is a slip risk and a moisture problem in one.

Slab foundations and soil movement

Houston's clay soils swell when wet and shrink in drought, and slab homes can shift over time. A walk-in tub has to sit level for the door to seal and the water to drain. A careful installer checks level, shims and supports the tub as the manufacturer directs, and leaves an access panel where the design allows, so a future plumber can reach the drain without tearing out walls.

A storm-season checklist for an accessible bathroom

  1. Know where the main water shutoff is, and make sure someone besides the person bathing can reach it.
  2. Store a flashlight or lantern in the bathroom, plus a nonslip mat for outside the tub.
  3. Write down how the tub drains without power, and whether the water heater needs electricity.
  4. Photograph the bathroom after installation, for insurance records.
  5. If the home floods, have the tub, wiring and wall cavity inspected before using jets or heaters again.

Questions we hear about this

Can I use a walk-in tub during a power outage?

You can still open the door, sit on the seat and drain by gravity, but jets, heated seats, in-line heaters and electric water heaters will not work. Ask whether your model drains without its pump.

Should I worry about mold with a walk-in tub in Houston?

Humidity raises the risk for any bathroom. A vented exhaust fan, well-sealed surround, a dried door gasket and jets that purge their lines all help. The EPA suggests keeping indoor humidity below 60 percent.

Does the City of Houston flood rule affect a tub replacement?

Usually not. The Chapter 19 elevation rule applies to new construction and substantial improvements in the floodplain. A larger remodel or addition might qualify, so ask the Houston Permitting Center.
